Trade Press, Companies and Networks

The printed and commercial infrastructure behind modern hairdressing.

← All research collections

Professional journals and advertising reveal how hairdressing became an increasingly connected industry. They record technical claims, training opportunities, product launches, professional debates and the emergence of international markets.

Trade journals as sources

A trade journal can preserve dated evidence of demonstrations, products, salons, organisations and personalities. Its articles, advertisements and editorial positions must nevertheless be read in their commercial and historical context.

Companies and markets

Manufacturers, wholesalers and salon suppliers transformed techniques into products and services. Company histories reveal distribution, branding, training and the relationship between local workshops and international businesses.

What we examine

  • Specialist periodicals and their geographic circulation, editorial focus and advertising markets.
  • Advertisements for waving machines, preparations, hairpieces, tools and training.
  • Manufacturer catalogues, business records, patents and trademarks.
  • Trade associations, exhibitions, training institutions and commercial networks.

From advertisement to historical evidence

  • An advertisement is evidence that a company made a particular statement at a particular time. It is not independent proof that the advertised performance or claimed priority was true.
  • Comparing dated advertisements with patents, surviving objects, corporate records and reports can help reconstruct product development and commercial reach.
  • Where only a scan or a later transcription survives, provenance and uncertainty should be made explicit.

Trade journals as historical evidence

Professional knowledge in print

Trade journals circulated technical instructions, salon reports, advertisements, debates, product announcements and accounts of demonstrations. Their intended professional audience shaped what they chose to publish.

A London point of reference

The Hairdressers’ Weekly Journal of October 1906 is an important lead for examining contemporary reporting around Nessler’s London demonstration. Original wording and page context should be checked before quotation.

Reading across languages

English, German and French periodicals can describe the same invention differently. Comparing terminology, dates and images helps identify transmission, translation and marketing changes.

Companies, products and markets

From invention to distribution

Manufacturers, wholesalers, training schools and salons connected technical developments to consumers. Patents alone cannot reveal the scale of this commercial network.

Nestlé-LeMur

The 1928 combination of C. Nestle and Le Mur offers a case study in company history, brands, product portfolios and the American beauty industry. Individual product claims should be tied to dated advertisements or catalogues.

Advertising as evidence

An advertisement can document a name, claim, date, price, address or visual representation. It cannot by itself prove the claimed effectiveness, market dominance or invention priority.

A method for describing periodicals

Bibliographic record

Record the exact title, issue date, volume, page, publication place, language and holding institution.

Item-level evidence

Describe the article or advertisement separately from the journal issue; transcribe names, product marks and dates exactly, preserving uncertainties.

Rights and access

Distinguish public-domain sources, licensed reproductions, excerpts and bibliographic references. Link to institutional holdings where full reproduction is not authorised.

Joyner’s 1928 patent: tracing an invention through the beauty industry

Marjorie Stewart Joyner’s patent offers a documented starting point for studying the relationship between hairdressing inventions, vocational education and the American beauty business. The application was filed in May 1928 and the patent granted in November of that year.

Different records answer different questions

Patent specifications establish the inventor, assignee, claims and illustrated mechanism. Trade journals and advertisements may show how equipment was marketed. Training manuals and school records can demonstrate what practitioners were taught. Surviving salon equipment can reveal actual use. These categories should not be treated as interchangeable evidence.

The Walker network

Joyner’s professional association with the Madam C. J. Walker Manufacturing Company makes its schools, commercial publications and professional contacts important leads. The Smithsonian documents her significant training role; evidence for the particular machine’s distribution must still be identified in contemporary sources.

Open archival questions

Can we locate a dated advertisement for the patented apparatus? Was it sold under a particular trade name? Do school manuals or salon photographs show the exact patented configuration? These questions remain open until contemporary documentation is found.

Source trail: US Patent 1,693,515 · National Archives patent drawings · Smithsonian historical context.

How a technique becomes a product: Grateau, Mayer and the permanent wave

The packaging for a Marcel-style ondulation iron is an example of object evidence: it shows how a named technique could be presented and explained to purchasers. To establish a date, distribution network or original manufacturer, it must be paired with catalogues, trade advertisements and surviving business records.

Josef Mayer and patent-to-market research

US Patent 1,619,794 establishes a particular technical design in 1927, not how many units were sold. Contemporary advertisements for Mayer Realistic, catalogues, professional teaching material and company registrations are the appropriate next sources for commercial history.

International comparison

The project’s existing survey identifies further systems associated with Gallia, Szeemann, Suter, Calvete, Frederics, Racine and Willatt. For each, the research record should separate the inventor, company or trade name, patent dates, advertised claims and evidence of actual salon use. This prevents later accounts from turning commercial language into unsupported invention priorities.
