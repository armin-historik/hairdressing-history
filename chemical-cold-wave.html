What changed with the cold wave?

Early permanent waving relied on combinations of winding, chemical preparation and substantial external heat. Chemical cold-wave systems took a different route: a reducing solution altered bonds within the hair, allowing the wound hair to be reshaped without the large electrical heating apparatus associated with earlier salon machines.

“Cold” does not mean that no chemistry is involved. It means that the process can operate without the intense external heat of traditional hot permanent-waving equipment. The distinction is important: the cold wave was a change in the method of reshaping hair, not simply a more efficient heater.

Inside the hair: the chemical principle

Hair contains keratin proteins. Some keratin chains are connected by disulfide bonds, formed between sulfur-containing cysteine residues. These bonds help stabilise hair shape. In a typical chemical cold-wave process, a reducing agent breaks some of these bonds; the hair is held around rods in a new shape; an oxidising neutraliser then helps form disulfide bonds again.

Keratin–S–S–Keratin → reduction → Keratin–SH + HS–Keratin → oxidation → Keratin–S–S–Keratin

This is a simplified reaction scheme, not a complete formulation or salon instruction. Reducing agents, pH, processing conditions and neutralising chemistry varied between historical products.

Arnold Frederick Willat (1886–1988)

Arnold F. Willat was an American electrical engineer associated with the development and commercialisation of chemical cold waving. His obituary reports that he had earlier developed an electrically operated hot permanent-wave machine at a manufacturer's request. The maker, model and exact date of that earlier machine remain unidentified.

Willat's career therefore connects two technical eras. However, his work on an electrical machine does not establish that he invented the earlier hot-wave method. His cold-wave patent claims must also be distinguished from earlier research into chemical changes in keratin.

Biographical evidence: A US Social Security death-index entry reproduced in family-history databases gives 27 January 1886 as his birth date and 7 March 1988 as his death date. These details should be checked against the underlying record before being treated as independently verified archival evidence.

The Willat Company and salon practice

The Willat Company of San Francisco manufactured and sold cold-wave solutions and salon-related supplies for decades. A 1988 obituary reports that the company discontinued production of cold-wave solutions in 1987; it does not by itself establish that the entire company closed that year. Curl papers and dispensers associated with the Willat name were also reported in salon use.

Changing from hot-wave apparatus to chemical cold-wave products altered salon equipment, working methods and the commercial supply chain. The history of the company is therefore part of the history of professional hairdressing, not merely a footnote to an invention.

The reported company address at 1077 Howard Street, the attribution of particular dryer models and the details of patent litigation require dated directory entries, product advertisements or court records before they can be presented as established facts.

Selected chronology: from keratin research to cold-wave products

1934

John Bamber Speakman's British keratin-treatment patent family claims priority from 10 December 1934. Priority dates do not by themselves establish first commercial use.

1937

Arnold F. Willat files US 2,180,380 on 8 March and US 2,266,111 on 26 June.

1939

US 2,180,380 is granted on 21 November. Its claims include ammonium hydrosulfide and hydrogen sulfide solutions: it must not be presented as proof that Willat's patented formula was already ammonium thioglycolate.

1941

US 2,266,111 is granted on 16 December, describing chemical waving and related salon procedures.

c. 1945

The surviving Charm-Kurl home cold-wave kit illustrates the expanding market beyond any single inventor or manufacturer.

1951

Everett G. McDonough's US 2,577,710 is granted, documenting further work on chemical waving compositions.

1987–1988

According to Willat's obituary, his company stopped manufacturing cold-wave solutions in 1987; Willat died in March 1988.

This is a chronology of selected documents and objects, not a complete chronology of first inventions or commercial introductions.
