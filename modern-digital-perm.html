What the modern equipment does

Contemporary digital-perm systems combine chemical treatment with electrically heated rods and electronic control of temperature and timing. Designs vary by manufacturer and market.

The striking resemblance

Both Nessler’s early approach and modern hot-perm systems combine winding, chemicals and heat. The similarity is a meaningful comparison of technical principles, not proof of an unbroken lineage of machines.

What has changed

Modern apparatus may include compact heaters, timers and electronic controls; chemistry, salon workflow and safety requirements also differ. A comparison should use manufacturer documentation for a named current model.

An open historical question

Did modern developers consciously draw on early permanent-wave technology, or independently employ a similar physical principle? That requires direct evidence such as design histories, patent citations or inventor statements.

A system, not a single machine

The expression digital perm is used for modern hot-perming services in which chemical processing is combined with heated rods and electronically controlled equipment. It is a trade term, not the name of one universally standardised invention. The word digital refers to the control equipment; it does not mean that hair is reshaped by a digital process.

1 · Hair preparation

Hair is assessed and wound onto rods. Rod geometry, section size and wrapping influence the resulting arrangement.

2 · Chemical processing

Products change the hair’s internal bonding conditions so that it can be reshaped. Exact formulas and procedures depend on the product and should not be generalised.

3 · Controlled heating

Electrically heated rods and electronic controls manage the thermal stage. The permitted settings depend on the equipment and product instructions.

Neutralisation or fixing is a separate essential part of the chemical process. This historical account is not an operating guide; temperatures, exposure times and chemical sequences must not be inferred from a generic description.

Historical caution: A shared combination of heat, chemistry and winding demonstrates a technical resemblance. It does not establish that a modern manufacturer copied Nessler’s apparatus or that an uninterrupted engineering lineage can be documented.

Why this is not simply a return to the old hot wave

Twentieth-century cold-wave chemistry made permanent waving possible without the large heated installations associated with earlier methods. Modern digital-perm services reintroduce a controlled heating stage within a different technical and chemical environment. The history therefore involves changing combinations of methods, not a straightforward sequence in which one machine replaces another.

Documented machines and manufacturers

The history of the modern digital perm is not the history of one universally acknowledged inventor. Manufacturer records, product listings and patent documents allow us to identify specific developments without claiming that any one of them was the world's first digital perm.

CREATE · South Korea

CREATE, established in 1991, reports developing a Quick Setting Permanent Device in 1998. This is a manufacturer's historical claim and should not be treated as independent proof of global priority. Later named machines include Twin Belle and Swing Belle, dated to 2002 in a former UK distributor's historical account. A manufacturer's archived product listing for a heat-wave perm machine also describes ceramic rods and a digital touch-control panel.

Sources: CREATE company profile, tradeKorea; former UK distributor's machine archive; CREATE equipment listing.

OOHIRO · Japan

OOHIRO's ODIS series is an identifiable family of digital-perm machines. Crucially, the ODIS-2 is named as the heating equipment in a published European patent document concerning permanent reshaping of keratin fibres. This provides independent technical documentation that the machine was used in an experimental procedure; it does not establish when the ODIS family was first invented.

Primary technical source: EP 2 512 424 B1, European Patent Office. See also EP 2 198 737 A1.

PAIMORE · Japan

PAIMORE markets a Shape Memory Digital Perm system. Brand and patent claims should be verified separately against the official trademark and patent registers. In particular, Japanese patent JP 4224462 B2, sometimes associated with discussions of digital-perm equipment, lists Fuji Co., Ltd. as its original assignee. It must not be attributed to PAIMORE without evidence of a later transfer or other documented relationship.

Patent register: JP 4224462 B2, bibliographic data and drawings.

How to establish the modern history

To date the emergence of digital-perm technology responsibly, the project needs identifiable products and dated records. A useful source dossier for each model would include the manufacturer, model designation, date of documentation, market, heating and control specifications, the exact source, and whether the evidence concerns a prototype, patent, commercial product or salon service.

  • Patents: distinguish the filing date, publication date and claims from evidence of actual manufacture.
  • Manuals and catalogues: identify a specific marketed apparatus and the functions advertised at that date.
  • Trade journals: establish when terminology and professional practices appeared in a given country.
  • Photographs and surviving equipment: document physical configurations, ideally with dated provenance.

Four terms that should not be confused

Hot perm

A broad description of permanent waving that includes an applied heating stage. It does not by itself identify a specific machine or formulation.

Cold wave

A chemically based permanent-wave method that does not require the earlier type of externally heated waving apparatus. See Chapter 05.

Digital perm

A contemporary trade expression generally associated with heated rods and electronic controls. Its precise meaning can vary by market and brand.

Setting versus perming

Temporary heat styling and chemically lasting reshaping are not equivalent processes, even when both use heated tools.

Historical sequence: continuity and change

Early twentieth century · Electrically heated waving

Nessler's pioneering work combined hair winding, chemical preparation and heat. Later apparatus varied in form and method. See Chapter 01 and Chapter 04.

Mid-twentieth century · Chemical cold waving

Cold-wave systems reduced reliance on the large heated apparatus of earlier salons and broadened the technical options available to hairdressers. See Chapter 05.

Later developments · Compact heat and electronic control

Contemporary hot-perm devices can use electrically heated rods with electronically managed settings. The date, inventor and first commercial introduction of any particular digital-perm system must be established from dated primary evidence.

Today · Multiple methods coexist

Cold-wave methods, heated permanent waving and temporary styling remain distinct categories. Modern equipment should be compared using documented model specifications, not advertising terminology alone.
