Mirrors, Combs and Brushes

Three everyday objects, many centuries of craft, industry and social history.

← All research collections

A mirror, a comb and a brush are so familiar that their history is easily overlooked. Yet each object brings together raw materials, specialist workshops, changing fashions, trade routes and the everyday routines of hairdressers and their clients.

The mirror

Mirrors shaped the arrangement of salons and the relationship between practitioner and client. Their history involves glassmaking, reflective coatings, furniture, lighting and the changing experience of seeing oneself.

The comb and the brush

Combs and brushes served different functions: separating, arranging, detangling, cleaning and finishing hair. Their forms depend on materials, methods of manufacture and the work for which they were designed.

Three objects, three histories

  • Mirrors: from polished reflective surfaces to glass mirrors with metal coatings, and from hand mirrors to fitted salon stations.
  • Combs: wood, horn, bone, hard rubber, celluloid and later synthetic materials; hand cutting, moulding and mass production.
  • Brushes: wooden or other handles, natural or manufactured bristles, drilling, setting and mechanised brushmaking.

The workshop behind the object

  • Materials did not merely change the appearance of a tool. They influenced price, durability, cleaning, repair and the possibility of large-scale production.
  • Regional craft traditions and specialised manufacturers deserve attention alongside well-known hairdressing brands. Todtnau and the surrounding Black Forest are particularly interesting for the history of brushmaking.
  • For each object, the project will distinguish documented manufacture, advertisements, patents, surviving examples and later interpretations.

The mirror: manufacture and meaning

From reflective surfaces to modern mirrors

The history of mirrors involves changes in glassmaking, metal coatings and workshop techniques. Historical silvered and mercury-based mirrors require careful distinction; their production and conservation raise different technical and health questions.

The salon mirror

A mirror was more than a domestic accessory: its placement affected lighting, the customer’s view, the working position of the hairdresser and the design of the salon. Photographs and equipment catalogues can document these arrangements.

Research material

Historical literature on silvering and mercury mirrors provides technical context. Individual claims about dates, manufacturers or inventions must be checked against the original editions.

The comb: material, craft and industry

Material transitions

Horn, bone, wood, tortoiseshell, hard rubber and early plastics belong to different production traditions. Their chronology varies by region and market; no single material replaced all others at once.

Making a comb

Sawing, cutting teeth, shaping, polishing and finishing can be examined through tools, factory records, patents and surviving objects. The width and spacing of teeth also reflect different uses.

A Swiss research connection

The history of comb manufacture in Mümliswil offers a possible comparative case for the industrial and regional study of comb-making. Specific company and production claims require individual source references.

The brush: from workshop to factory

Materials and construction

A brush combines a handle or stock, bristles or other fibres, and a method of fastening. Material selection and the arrangement of bristles help distinguish manufacture and intended use.

Regional manufacturing

The Todtnau area and the wider Black Forest offer important leads for brush-making history. Local craft traditions, factory development and specialist machinery should be investigated through municipal and company archives.

Machines and patents

Historical brush-making machinery and firms such as Zahoransky open a path into mechanisation. Dates of particular machines and technical milestones must be supported by original company records, patents or catalogues.

Shared questions for the three objects

Who made them?

Identify workshops, manufacturers, suppliers and trade routes rather than treating the objects as anonymous accessories.

Who used them?

Compare professional salon equipment with domestic objects, considering price, hygiene, fashion, gender and working conditions.

What survives?

Bring together museum objects, makers’ marks, catalogues, advertisements, photographs and patents, with clear provenance.

Questions for the collection

  • What did a working hairdresser use in a particular decade, and what did clients use at home?
  • How did industrial materials change the price and accessibility of combs and brushes?
  • Which firms supplied mirrors, combs and brushes to professional salons rather than household customers?

Salon tools and specialised apparatus

Mirrors, combs and brushes were everyday working tools; permanent-waving machines belonged to a different category of specialised equipment. Studying their use together can nevertheless illuminate the organisation of a salon and the changing relationship between manual skill and mechanical apparatus.

Marjorie Stewart Joyner’s 1928 design is an example: its overhead dome supported several electrically heated curling irons, while separate clamps reduced the weight borne by the client’s hair. The patent also illustrated scalp protection during winding.

See the full English-language discussions in Technology & Patents and Pioneers & People, and consult the original US patent and drawings.

OBJECT STUDY · HEATED TONGS

The Marcel iron: an object, a method and a market

Marcel waving illustrates why an implement must be studied alongside the hand technique used with it. Heated tongs did not produce the wave automatically: temperature, pressure, direction and timing were all matters of professional skill.

What the packaging can tell us

The surviving package offers evidence of product presentation and printed directions. Its language, manufacturer’s marks and construction should be transcribed and compared with dated advertisements before assigning a production date. It is not itself a patent for the original invention.

For the distinction between temporary thermal waving and permanent chemical waving, see Technology & Patents: Marcel Grateau. The patented electric heater for flat winding is examined separately under Josef Mayer.

Reading objects as historical evidence. A surviving tool can document its construction and materials. Its date, maker, intended users and everyday adoption require separate evidence such as a marked object, dated catalogue, trade advertisement, workshop record or patent. A patent drawing does not alone establish that a tool was manufactured or sold.
