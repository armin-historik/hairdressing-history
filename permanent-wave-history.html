There was never just one invention

The permanent wave emerged through different approaches to shaping hair, applying heat, developing chemical treatments and making salon equipment practical. An inventor’s patent, a company’s machine and a widely adopted technique are not the same historical claim.

Marcel Grateau

Heated tongs produced a temporary wave. This important precursor must be distinguished from later lasting, chemically assisted treatments.

Karl Ludwig (Charles) Nessler

His early permanent-waving process combined winding, chemical preparation and heat. The London demonstration of 1906 is a central milestone.

Winding methods and Josef Mayer

Different winding geometries posed new challenges for heating devices. Mayer’s US patent 1,619,794 describes a specific appliance arrangement.

Marjorie Stewart Joyner

Her 1928 patent addressed the arrangement and support of multiple heated irons. It solved a different design problem from Mayer’s appliance.

Read the inventions in their original drawings

Patent drawings allow us to examine the arrangement of parts rather than relying only on later retellings. Two inventions from the 1920s illustrate why the history cannot be reduced to a single machine.

Marjorie Stewart Joyner, 1928

Joyner's patent focuses on the support and positioning of multiple heated implements. Its mechanical arrangement should be assessed independently from Mayer's appliance.

How to use this research

Start with the historical overview, follow the relevant specialist chapter, and consult the primary document. Distinguish patent date, public demonstration, manufacture and commercial adoption; these are different kinds of evidence. Where attribution or influence remains unproven, the research register identifies the gap.

Our research principle: Every claim should be traceable to an identifiable document. Patents demonstrate claimed inventions, not necessarily commercial success or priority over all earlier practice. Open questions are marked as such.
