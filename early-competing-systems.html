Several solutions to a shared challenge

The early permanent-wave industry did not develop through one uninterrupted sequence of machines. Designers, practitioners and manufacturers addressed different problems: how to wind hair, supply and distribute heat, position equipment safely, and make a treatment workable in a salon.

Important distinction: the people and trade names below are research leads. Their presence here does not establish an invention date, a patent priority, a successful product or a direct connection to Nessler.

Gallia: an early French system requiring documentation

Gallia is mentioned in accounts of competing early French permanent-wave systems. At present, the identification of a specific machine configuration, manufacturer and technical innovation is not secure enough to present as established fact.

Before describing its construction, the research needs a contemporary illustrated catalogue, dated advertisement, technical report or patent that links the name Gallia to a particular apparatus.

Open question: Was Gallia primarily a branded salon procedure, a manufactured apparatus, or both? The available identification must be tested against period sources.

Status: historical identification provisional; technical attribution not verified.

Étienne Szeemann: professional involvement versus invention

Étienne Szeemann appears as a research lead in the professional environment of early permanent waving. His role in that network should not be mistaken for proof of a particular patented machine or a demonstrable technical improvement.

A reliable technical profile requires a dated specification, drawing, trade-journal description or comparable contemporary record naming Szeemann and explaining what was changed. Claims concerning temperature regulation, winding or apparatus design should remain provisional until such evidence is located.

Open question: What precisely did Szeemann contribute, and is the contribution documented as a method, an apparatus, a commercial system or professional practice?

Status: professional/historical lead; specific invention not independently established.

Eugène François Suter: patented apparatus and heating

Eugène François Suter belongs to the documented history of early permanent-wave apparatus. British Patent GB 111372 (published in 1917) and United States Patent US 1,266,879 (issued in 1918) provide identifiable primary documents for his technical work. Their descriptions, illustrations and claims offer a firmer starting point than retrospective inventor lists.

The existence of these patents establishes a documented technical proposal. It does not, on its own, establish how many machines were manufactured, which salons adopted them, or whether every advertised “Eugene” machine embodied the same construction. Patent disputes involving the Eugene business and Nestlé-LeMur also show why the industrial and legal history of this technology merits separate examination.

Original documents: GB 111372 (1917) ↗ · US 1,266,879 (1918) ↗
Research caution: Do not confuse Eugène François Suter with Marcel J. Suter, whose name appears in a different, later patent record.

Status: identifiable patent documents; detailed claim-by-claim interpretation and evidence of commercial adoption still to be completed.

Isidoro Calvete and the Icall machine

The Icall machine is an important lead for the history of manufactured permanent-wave equipment. A historical photograph associated with the Calvete family offers a visual record of a machine identified as Icall. Its descriptive metadata supplies a provenance lead that can be followed independently.

A photograph can help identify the machine’s overall arrangement, but it cannot by itself prove that Isidoro Calvete invented every component, establish a patent priority, or measure commercial distribution. Those questions require catalogues, dated advertisements, business records and patent documents.

Photographic source: Icall Machine 1920 — Wikimedia Commons ↗. Consult the file page for attribution, licensing and the exact provenance statement before reproducing the photograph.

Status: historical photographic lead; inventor/manufacturer attribution and commercial reach remain open.

From a name to a documented machine

Each future technical profile should identify the exact source (patent number, archive reference or periodical issue), date, inventor and applicant, illustrated mechanical arrangement, claimed novelty, and, separately, any evidence of manufacture or use. Where no original apparatus image is available, we should not substitute a visually similar machine without labelling it.
