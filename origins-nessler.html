The central historical question

What did Nessler actually contribute?

Long before permanent waving, hairdressers could shape hair temporarily with heated irons. Nessler’s importance is associated with an early method intended to produce a longer-lasting wave by treating hair while it was wound and heated. These are different historical and technical categories: a heat-set hairstyle is not a chemically assisted permanent wave.

To evaluate a claim of invention, we must distinguish a documented public demonstration, a patent claim, a commercial apparatus and later recollections. They are related sources, but they answer different questions.

Before Nessler: heated shaping

Marcel Grateau’s heated waving iron offered temporary shaping. It is a relevant technical precursor, not a chemical permanent wave. A temporary wave and a permanent wave must not be treated as the same invention.

The combination of principles

Nessler’s early permanent-wave method combined winding, chemical treatment and heat. The publicly documented London demonstration in October 1906 is a useful chronological anchor. The frequently quoted year 1905 for the machine should be distinguished from this documented demonstration.

A working chronology

Before 1906 · Temporary heat shaping. Marcel-style heated irons formed waves, but did not establish the same durable chemical transformation sought by permanent-wave methods.

8 October 1906 · London. A professional trade-journal reference provides a contemporary anchor for Nessler’s public presentation. The exact claims made at the event must be checked against the original article.

1909 onward · Patenting and adaptation. Later patent records provide evidence of specific claimed apparatus or process features. Their filing dates and drawings should not be projected backward onto the 1906 demonstration.

1920s onward · Competing technical approaches. Flat winding, alternative heaters and salon-machine arrangements show that permanent waving became a field of distinct engineering problems rather than one unchanged device.

The practical engineering challenge

Heat had to be applied while the hair was wound, without damaging the client or operator. Apparatus weight, insulation, treatment time and the placement of heating units became important problems for later designers.

Separate the process from the machine

QuestionProcessApparatus
What is being changed?The treatment of wound hair through heat and chemical preparation.The practical means of winding, heating, supporting and insulating the treatment.
What can a patent establish?Specific claimed treatment steps, if present in the claims.Specific claimed components and arrangements shown in the document.
What remains separate?Whether a method was used commercially, and with what results.Whether the illustrated machine was manufactured, sold or used in a salon.

This distinction helps explain why later inventors could innovate without necessarily inventing the entire permanent-wave process anew.

What Nessler set in motion

Nessler did not define one unchanging machine model. The underlying permanent-waving problem opened a market for alternative devices, winding geometries, salon procedures and later chemistry.

Evidence and source criticism

The research archive identifies a contemporary professional-journal reference for the London demonstration dated 8 October 1906. Its original page, issue context and wording should be reproduced or transcribed before making detailed claims about the machine shown that day.

Patents must be read by filing date, priority, jurisdiction and exact claims. A later patent drawing cannot automatically be presented as a photograph or design of the machine demonstrated in 1906.

Still to establish: a securely dated illustration of the demonstration apparatus; the exact formulation and working sequence used at the event; and evidence for how later commercial models differed from the original process.
