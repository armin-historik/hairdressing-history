Research Collection · Technology & Patents

Devices, Processes and Inventions

How hairdressing technologies developed, travelled and changed professional practice.

← All research collections

The history of hairdressing is also a history of technical experimentation. Heating, shaping, drying and treating hair required tools, materials, skilled operators and systems for teaching new methods. Patents offer valuable evidence, but they are only one part of the story.

From technique to apparatus

The transition from manual curling and heated implements to increasingly elaborate permanent-wave apparatus was not a single invention. Different practitioners and manufacturers developed competing approaches, improved components and adapted techniques to salon conditions.

Reading patents critically

A patent can document an inventor’s technical proposal and its claimed novelty. It does not, by itself, establish commercial production, widespread use or priority over every earlier practice.

Research themes

  • Heating and temperature control: the challenge of applying heat consistently while protecting hair and scalp.
  • Waving and setting: winding methods, clamps, rollers and their interaction with hair preparations.
  • Salon equipment: the practical organisation of machines, electrical supply and operator training.
  • Patent histories: national filings, later improvements, manufacturers and commercial names.

People, inventions and evidence

  • Karl Ludwig (Charles) Nessler is an important reference point for the development and international diffusion of permanent-wave technology. His life and documentary record are treated in detail by the separate Nessler Research Project.
  • The wider history also includes other inventors, practitioners and firms. Their technical contributions must be assessed against dated contemporary evidence rather than later promotional claims.
  • Useful primary sources include patent specifications, trade journals, advertisements, catalogues, instruction manuals and photographs of machines in actual salons.

A chronology of changing methods

1906 — London demonstration

Nessler’s London demonstration on 8 October 1906 is a key chronological reference for the early public presentation of his permanent-wave process. The event should be studied alongside contemporary trade reporting, rather than only through later retellings.

1909–1921 — Patenting and adaptation

Patent records in Switzerland, Britain, Germany and the United States trace a sequence of technical claims and adaptations. Examples under investigation include Swiss patent CH 49814 and US patent 1,400,370 (1921). A patent records a claimed invention, not proof of commercial manufacture.

1925–1927 — An American apparatus patent

The archival register records J. Mayer’s “Permanent Hair Waving Appliance”, US 1,619,794: filed 19 March 1925 and granted 1 March 1927. Its drawings offer a concrete starting point for comparison with other systems.

1928 onward — Industrial consolidation

The formation of Nestlé-LeMur in 1928 illustrates how the story extends beyond individual inventors to companies, product lines, distribution and salon training.

How to compare an apparatus

Mechanical arrangement

Identify the heating elements, clamps, winding direction, supports, insulation and points of contact with hair and scalp. Describe what the drawing actually shows before interpreting its function.

Claims versus practice

Compare the patent specification with advertisements, trade demonstrations, instruction manuals, photographs and surviving devices. A commercial product name should not be assigned unless a source supports it.

Safety and working conditions

Examine operating time, heat exposure, electrical infrastructure, operator training and the salon setting. Claims of safety or performance in period advertisements must be treated as promotional statements.

Open research questions

  • Which patented designs reached serial production, and under what commercial names?
  • How did salons adapt equipment to local electrical and safety conditions?
  • How did the relationships among inventors, manufacturers, demonstrators and training schools change over time?

ARCHIVAL CASE STUDY · 1928

Case study: Marjorie Stewart Joyner’s permanent-waving machine (1928)

In 1928, American hairdresser and educator Marjorie Stewart Joyner received US Patent 1,693,515, titled Permanent Waving Machine. Its drawings depict an arrangement for treating multiple sections of hair around the head. The patent offers a useful comparison with earlier approaches to heated permanent waving.

What the patent helps establish

The patent is evidence of a specific technical proposal and its claimed construction. It can be examined for the arrangement of heating elements, hair holders and supporting structures. It does not, by itself, establish how many machines were manufactured, how widely they were adopted, or precisely how much treatment time they saved.

Comparing Nessler, Mayer and Joyner

The collection already includes J. Mayer’s US Patent 1,619,794 (1927). Read alongside Joyner’s patent and Nessler’s earlier work, these documents permit a comparison of mechanisms, claims and technical priorities without treating the development of permanent waving as the achievement of one inventor alone.

Primary document and research note

The existing Nessler research site reproduces Joyner’s patent drawings and discusses her machine, its proposed use and her professional career. Read the illustrated Joyner research article and patent references ↗
